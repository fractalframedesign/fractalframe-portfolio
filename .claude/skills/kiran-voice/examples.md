# Annotated excerpts — imitate these

Real passages from Kiran's book manuscript. Match this register, not a description of it.

## Cold open (second person, no throat-clearing)

> Maybe you chose a layout because it felt clean. Picked a label because it seemed obvious. Cut a feature because you could not imagine anyone needing it. You called that instinct. You might even have called it experience. A lot of the time, it was just you designing for yourself in a room full of people who are nothing like you.

Note: fragments ("Picked a label..."), spelled-out "could not", the turn lands in the last sentence.

## Research translation

> In 1977, Lee Ross, David Greene, and Pamela House at Stanford ran a series of experiments on what they called the false consensus effect. They gave people ordinary, unremarkable scenarios (a speeding ticket, a class assignment, a TV commercial) and asked them to predict what most other people would do. [...] In plain terms, you assume most people would do what you would do. And when they do not, you tend to treat them as the exception.
>
> That second part matters as much as the first.

Note: concrete scenario list in parentheses, "In plain terms" translation, then the second-order finding gets its own paragraph.

## Case study voice (sympathy, then the turn)

> In May 2009, Google announced Google Wave and the room loved it. The product mixed email, chat, live editing, and social updates into one thing. The people building it were not fools. Lars and Jens Rasmussen had already built Google Maps. They knew how to ship hard products.
>
> Then Wave opened to 100,000 users in September 2009 and normal people hit a wall. They did not know what it was for.

Note: exact dates and numbers, short sentences, "Then" as the pivot.

## Inner monologue as period-questions

> A lot of people opened it and did not get it. Not in some grand product sense. In the basic sense. What is this for. Why is this showing me other people's photos. Why would I keep using it.

Note: questions with periods — intentional. Fragments building to the monologue.

## Personal admission

> For the first few years, I kept my master's in psychology quiet. I was almost embarrassed by it, like I was a psychologist pretending to be a designer. I had it completely backwards.

Note: brief, unglamorous, ends on a blunt self-verdict. No lesson extracted — the reader gets it.

## Work story (specific, one person, one moment)

> Then I noticed more frames in the file. When I asked about them she waved them off. Too crazy, probably not what we were looking for. We looked anyway. The explorations were better. Not slightly better. They solved the actual problem in a way the safe version didn't.

Note: reported speech without quotation marks ("Too crazy, probably not what we were looking for"), escalation through fragments.

## The advice register (one question, honest limits)

> So before you cut the stronger direction down, ask one question: am I changing this because the evidence says it is wrong, or because I am scared to be the person who backed it?
>
> That question is not magic. It just stops you from treating your own ease as evidence.

## Closers (aphorism, then stop)

> The most dangerous thing in design is not getting it wrong. It is getting it wrong while feeling dead certain you got it right.

> A product that only works for people who think like you is a mirror with buttons on it.

> The fraud feeling is not going away. What matters is whether it makes you smaller or sharper. The user never sees your personal struggle. They see the decision that came out of it.

Note: reframe, not summary. Nothing follows the punchline.
