# Sentence-level voice

## Rhythm

- Default sentence is short and declarative. "The output changed." "It worked." "That is a normal blind spot."
- Deliberate fragments for emphasis, often in runs of two or three: "Same party. Same street. Same room." / "Not after a tour. Not on a second try. Right now." / "No familiar starting point. No simple handle."
- Repetition as rhythm: start consecutive sentences with the same frame. "Someone assumed users would... Someone assumed people would... Someone assumed that if..."
- "That is..." as a sentence opener to name what just happened: "That is processing fluency." "That is where the work starts shrinking." Used constantly; keep it.
- Long sentences exist but are rare and always followed by a short one that lands the point.

## Distinctive habits (keep these — they are the fingerprint)

- **Questions written as statements with periods**, especially when voicing someone's inner monologue: "What is this for. Why is this showing me other people's photos. Why would I keep using it." This is intentional, not an error. Use it when narrating confusion or a self-check.
- **Spelled-out negations in formal register**: "do not", "cannot", "does not", "is not" carry the weight of the argument. Contractions ("I've", "didn't", "couldn't") appear in personal asides and stories. This mix is deliberate: formal for claims, contracted for confession.
- **Naming the move**: after describing a behavior, he names it plainly. "Both groups are doing the same thing." "That is usually when the trouble starts."
- **Honest hedges that concede ground before pressing on**: "Sometimes that is true." "That would be too simple." "This does not mean your instincts are worthless." He steel-mans the objection in one short sentence, then continues.
- **Aphoristic closers**: chapters and major sections end on a compressed, quotable line. "A product that only works for people who think like you is a mirror with buttons on it." "The user never sees your personal struggle. They see the decision that came out of it." One per section maximum; never explain the aphorism after saying it.
- **Personal admissions in first person**, brief and unglamorous: "I still catch myself doing this." "I've done this too." "I had it completely backwards." One or two per chapter. They come early or mid-argument, never as the conclusion.

## Vocabulary

- Plain Anglo-Saxon verbs: gets, keeps, starts, holds, lands, ships, shrinks, leaks, sticks. Not: facilitates, utilizes, demonstrates, encompasses.
- Almost no stacked adjectives. One adjective max, and it works hard: "the timid version", "a ready-made excuse", "tidy excuse".
- Colloquial but precise: "the ugly part", "with any bite", "falls apart", "breathing the same air too long".
- Money and numbers stated exactly: "$41 million", "100,000 users", "more than 200 studies", "May 2009".

## Grammar notes (ESL — fix silently, never flag)

- Articles (a/an/the) and preposition choices may slip in drafts; correct them without comment.
- Do not "correct" the intentional patterns above (period-questions, fragments, spelled-out negations) — those are voice, not errors.
- Keep his syntax simple when fixing; never upgrade a plain sentence into a subordinate-clause chain.

## Never

- Em-dash chains or semicolon-heavy sentences. He uses parentheses occasionally for lists of examples: "(a speeding ticket, a class assignment, a TV commercial)".
- Exclamation marks. Hype. Superlatives without evidence.
- "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "At the end of the day", "It's important to note".
- Explaining a punchline. If the line lands, stop.
