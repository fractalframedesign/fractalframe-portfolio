# How Kiran builds a chapter or article

The arc is consistent. Problems arrive in this order, and so does the writing:

## 1. Title + subtitle

- Title is short and slightly confrontational, aimed at the reader: "Nobody Thinks Like You", "You Ruin Your Own Designs", "Your Mind Lies to You", "Taste Isn't Talent".
- Subtitle is one plain line naming the mechanism: "How false consensus makes you design for yourself".

## 2. Opening summary — vary it, never template it

Book chapters state the whole argument up front in one to three blunt sentences. For standalone articles, vary the opening so a reader moving between articles never sees the same device twice in a row. Options, roughly in order of preference:

- No summary at all — go straight into the cold open (the default when the cold open is strong)
- A single italic line stating the core claim: "*The action happens before you see a screen. Everything in this article follows from that one sentence.*"
- A bold aphorism as the first sentence of the opening paragraph: "**You cannot screenshot a conversation.**"
- A labeled "**In short:**" block — use sparingly, at most one article in a set

Never "TL;DR" — Kiran's readers include non-native English speakers, so avoid internet abbreviations. Whatever the form: no teasing, the summary states the argument, it does not advertise it.

## 3. Cold open — the reader's own behavior

Start inside the reader's head, second person, describing something they have done: "Maybe you chose a layout because it felt clean." or "You have been inside the design file for too long." No throat-clearing, no context-setting. Within the first section, one short first-person admission: "I still catch myself doing this."

## 4. The research

- Named researchers, institution or year, what they actually did in concrete terms: "In 1977, Lee Ross, David Greene, and Pamela House at Stanford ran a series of experiments..."
- One direct quote from the paper, set off with attribution.
- Immediately translated: "In plain terms, you assume most people would do what you would do."
- Then the part everyone misses: "That second part matters as much as the first." He always finds the second-order finding.
- A second or third supporting study appears later, casually, by surname only: "Bosveld, Koomen, and van der Pligt found the same pattern."

## 5. The case study — a real product failure

Color, Google Wave, MySpace. Real dates, real dollar amounts, real user numbers. Told with sympathy for the builders ("The people building it were not fools"), then the turn: what the bias did to them. Ends by scaling down: "I have seen smaller versions of this in product work over and over." Sometimes a personal work story instead (the Degreed designer's hidden frames) — specific, one person, one moment.

## 6. What to do — one question, one test

- Never a numbered checklist. One hard question to ask yourself: "Am I changing this because the evidence says it is wrong, or because I am scared to be the person who backed it?"
- One concrete test: the first-timer test, narrating the screen out loud, naming whose preference you are acting on.
- An honest limit: "That question is not magic." "Knowing about the bias does not protect you from it."

## 7. The closer

A short final paragraph ending on an aphorism. It reframes, it does not summarize. Then stop. Nothing after the punchline.

## Section headers

Lowercase-sentence style, plain, often verbs or claims: "False consensus in action", "What this costs", "You shrink the work first", "What the research says", "What to check". Never clever-for-clever's-sake, never questions.
