You have shipped something beautiful that nobody thanked you for. A transition you tuned for a week. A new feature the whole team was proud of. It went out, and the feedback was about something else. A login that failed. A page that would not load. A setting nobody could find.

You called that bad luck. Or bad timing. A lot of the time it was neither. You were working on a level your users had not reached yet.

I have done this more than once. It is an easy mistake to make, because the work at the top is the work that feels like design.

## What the research says

In 1943, Abraham Maslow published *A Theory of Human Motivation* in the journal *Psychological Review*. He described human needs as a set of levels: physiological needs first, then safety, then love, then esteem, then what he called self-actualization. His claim was about order. A need only starts to drive behavior once the needs beneath it are mostly met.

> It is quite true that man lives by bread alone, when there is no bread.

That is Maslow, in the 1943 paper.

In plain terms, nobody thinks about self-expression while they are hungry. The higher needs are real. They are just not available yet.

That is the part everyone remembers. The second part matters as much as the first.

Maslow did not say a level must be complete before the next one appears. He wrote that most people are partly satisfied and partly unsatisfied at every level at once, and he offered rough figures to make the point: someone might be 85 percent satisfied in physiological needs and only 10 percent in self-actualization. The levels overlap. The base does not need to be perfect. It needs to hold.

He never drew a pyramid either. Bridgman, Cummings, and Ballard traced the drawing in 2019 and found it came later, from management writing. The triangle is not his. The order is.

## Five levels of a product

In 2011, Aarron Walter redrew the idea for interfaces in *Designing for Emotion*. His version had four levels: functional, reliable, usable, pleasurable. I use a version with five, and I read it from the bottom up.

The first level is **minimal and functional**. The product does the one thing people came for. A person opens it with a job in mind and asks one question. Can I do it here. If the answer is no, nothing above this level exists for them.

The second is **reliable and stable**. It works every time. Not most of the time. A feature that fails one time in ten teaches people to brace before they tap. They save twice. They take screenshots as proof. That is not trust. That is a workaround.

Maslow would have called these two survival. Nobody praises them. People only mention them when they are missing.

The third level is **user-friendly**. Now people can stop surviving the product and start using it. Is it easy to learn. Is it hard to get wrong. A friendly interface does not make people feel clever. It makes them forget the interface.

The fourth is **polished**. Spacing that lines up, motion that explains, copy that sounds like a person. Polish is not decoration. It is evidence that someone cared about the parts nobody was supposed to notice. These two levels are psychological. They decide how the product feels.

The fifth is **new**. Something people have not seen before. A reason to come back, and a reason to tell someone. This is the level that gets the applause, which is why most teams want to start there.

## A billion dollars on the top floor

On April 6, 2020, Quibi launched. It had raised $1.75 billion. The people building it were not fools. Jeffrey Katzenberg had run the Disney studio and co-founded DreamWorks. Meg Whitman had run eBay and HP. They knew how to build large things.

The product had real novelty. Its signature feature, Turnstyle, let a show change its framing when you rotated the phone. The shows were expensive and looked it. The top two levels were funded generously.

Then normal people opened it and asked ordinary questions. Can I watch this on my television. Can I send this scene to a friend. At launch the answer to both was no. There was no way to cast to a TV, and the app blocked screenshots. The base was missing a job people already expected every video product to do.

On October 21, 2020, Quibi announced it was shutting down. A little over six months.

That would be too simple as a full explanation. The launch landed in the first weeks of a pandemic, and a product built for commutes met a world with no commutes. But the order still mattered. People will forgive a plain product that does the job. They do not stay for a clever one that does not.

I have seen smaller versions of this in product work over and over. When I worked on products used by hundreds of millions of people, the lower levels stopped being theory. A rare failure at that scale is a crowd.

## What to check

Before you start a piece of work, ask one question: which level is this for, and does the level below it hold?

Then run one test. Name the lowest level where the product still fails someone. Not the average person. Someone. A slow connection, an old phone, a first visit. If that level is below the one you are about to work on, you have your answer.

That question is not magic. It will not tell you what to build. It tells you what people are able to feel yet. And remember the second half of Maslow. The base does not have to be finished. Waiting for a perfect foundation is its own way of never shipping the good part.

People do not climb your pyramid. They stand on whatever level you finished.
